Pedestals are such an interesting word. By definition, they’re not remarkable:

> A pedestal is a physical base or support that holds up a statue, column, vase, or other heavy object
> 

But they you can also put things on pedestals. People, places, work, schools, the list goes on. It’s a very natural process but also such a fascinating thought space. 

## Schools

For those who don’t know, I’m from Bellevue Washington. It’s a city some 20 minutes east of Seattle known for the fact that there’s hella asians there. I do feel it’s a little shame the general reputation Bellevue has acquired. Rich asian tech bros. But also to be honest, it probably fits the bill. I think my high school was 60% asian. 

And as many people could’ve guessed, academic pressure was a norm. A lot of parents pushing their kids to get into a good college. It was probably middle school when people first brought up the idea of college aps and which college they wanted to go to. A lot of things were done with the end goal of college apps in mind. The general thinking was:

 get good test scores → get into a good college → make money → be happy?

I jokingly put “be happy” as the end step, because I do think I was genuinely happy at many moments in school, but also there was certain something to be said about how colleges were put on pedestals. People had put college as the end itself, rather than a means to an end. 

My senior year, one of my close friends had gotten into Stanford. Amazing school, but during Prom, he told us he felt he’d become one shrunk into being “the stanford kid” It was no longer about his personal goals or who he was as a person. He had achieved something that many people put on pedestals and had thus become an object of praise. What was stark was that nothing innately changed about him. but cause of some metrics, he was now “the stanford kid”.

I suppose there’s a bit of irony that I see now. I was one of those people who put colleges on a pedestal. My dream school was Wharton, and when I got rejected, I was worried if business was still for me. I think even now, there’s probably a little bit of an inferiority complex where I feel like I can’t compare in terms of colleges. But I do think there’s a lot of irony after going to college, I’ve still been able to get to the places that I’ve wanted to. There’s dumbasses at every college, and life isn’t magically better or worse because I go to one school instead of the other. 

## People

How often has the word “cracked” been thrown around? It’s a term I have a weird history with. For some context, I’m from Seattle, a huge tech hub, and, as you might imagine, there are a lot of people who have been programming for a while. In fact, I distinctly remember that in fifth grade, during one week of reading buddies, we hopped on [code.org](http://code.org) instead and taught them the basics of programming. Yet despite all that, I never got into programming. From age 11 or 12 until I was 16, I thought I was going to be a business major. I did business competitions, speech and debate, and attended finance summer programs. I eventually transitioned into CS in the fall of my junior year. To say I felt behind would be an understatement. Many of my peers had been programming for a decade longer than I had. In that context, I felt perpetually behind.

Then contrasted with once I started at Michigan. My freshman fall, I spent it uber locked in, mostly on recruiting. And I was super lucky, where I got an internship at Roblox my freshman year. I think the months after that were the most interesting. I almost immediately felt that I had been put on a pedestal. People who didn’t talk to me previously were now introducing themselves to me. I heard of people talking about me as a “CS wonder kid”. There was even a rumor at some point that I was dropping out after my freshman fall to work at Microsoft. I won’t lie, it definitely floated my ego, and for what it’s worth, there’s definitely a lot worse things to be known as than the uber smart cracked kid. But at the same time, I also felt like it was a lie. To myself, I still struggled in my CS classes. I went to office hours like everyone else. I stressed over exams, I failed homeworks etc. I wasn’t special in my head. Not the previous semester, and certainly not after getting my internship. There was a while that I spent unsure of how to proceed. I thought since some people would refer to me as cracked, I had to uphold this new standard that I had developed for myself in their heads.

For a while, I wanted to run away from that title. Still now, I feel the same as I did before all of this happened. I’m the same person that I was before. I still love my video games, I still struggle with classes, I still have bad mental health days where I feel like shit. But for a long time I wanted to have everyone see me as just another person because I felt like it would take the pressure off my shoulders of having to be special. But through one fateful dinner conversation with a friend who was slightly older, he shared an interesting point. That you can’t change how others view you, and in some ways, if others view you as special, in some ways you are special. I’m not saying that I am special, rather that most people who are considered special (with probably a very few number of exceptions) are just people under the hood. And them being special or cracked is a construct of social views towards them.

I think after hearing that, it helped take some of the pressure off myself. In some ways, I’ve stopped trying to constantly tell the world that I’m normal. I think the people closest to me know that, and with them I share my struggles that I go through. For others that may view me off a cursory glance off my Linkedin, they may view me as cracked or have some expectations for me, but that doesn’t define who I am. 

At the same time, I’m just as guilty as others. When I was in high school, I used to think everyone who made it to USAMO was a math god and was a natural born math genius. That was partially because nobody I knew had actually achieved USAMO. I used to view everyone who was USAMO as infallible. 

That went on a little bit of a tangent, but I think we all tend to easily put others on a pedestal. But in my experience, it’s a more nuanced picture than that some people are born different.

## Work

I think in a lot of similar ways as before, people also tend to put lines of work on pedestals. My personal connection is the quant industry. I think if you polled around most strong STEM universities’s engineering departments, chances are, people will have heard of quant. They might use terms like “genius”, “STEM”, “cracked”, or “olympiad” to describe people who work there. And to be fair, these companies have done a good job of marketing themselves. Jane Street sponsors numerous high school math competitions. All quant companies give out copious amount of merch to the point where some people’s entire outfit can be comprised of logos of various quant firms. They cultivate an aura of prestige and excellence, all topped off with jaw dropping first year salaries. And they’re not technically wrong in a lot of ways. In a summer, an intern at these companies can make more than their peers will make in an entire year. Yet despite all this, I don’t think the amount of reverence that is shown to these companies is justified.

I think there’s an extent to how the pedestals upon which these companies are put on, extend to the people working there (see entire previous section on putting people on pedestals). And funny enough, I do think there’s actually some bad in the whole process of putting these companies on a pedestal. I think people fail to see past the things that are put on pedestals a lot. For example, one topic that has come up a lot before, is whether or not choosing to stay in quant is the right move. I think for some people, they couldn’t wrap their heads around renegging a quant offer, or willingly turning it down to pursue something else. The emphasis put on how amazing these companies are makes it hard for some people to see past that. In a lot of ways, I think it’s hard for people not in the position to understand what someone going through that position is feeling; especially if that position involves something put on a pedestal.

## Closing Remarks

With all that said, I don’t think pedestals are inherently wrong. There’s probably some things that I still put on a pedestal that I shouldn’t. Money is one that easily comes to mind. But I think that as I’ve gone through my 2.5 years of college so far, it’s one of the things I seen time and time again. I hope that in the future, the things I put on pedestals are fewer and fewer and I can see the world through a level head; instead of looking up at a pedestal.
